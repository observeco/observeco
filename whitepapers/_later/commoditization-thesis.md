# The Commoditization Problem

## Why AI has made execution cheap — and differentiation the only defensible advantage left

**White Paper No. 1** · ObserveCo Consulting · Singapore
**Series:** *The Differentiation Playbook for Singapore Small Business*
**Evidence as of:** August 2026 · All figures confidence-labeled · Sources in §8

---

> **The thesis in one paragraph.** AI has collapsed the cost of starting and running a business, and it has commoditized the execution that used to separate winners from also-rans. When everyone can build a website, write the copy, run the ads, and answer the customer at near-zero marginal cost, the scarce resource is no longer effort — it is the answer to *"how do I stand out?"* And that answer goes stale every quarter, because the same AI that empowered you is empowering a new competitor every day. This paper assembles the Singapore evidence for that claim, and it is the foundation for everything that follows in this series.

---

## 1. The problem, stated plainly

For most of the history of small business, the barrier to entry was *execution*. You needed capital to build, skills to make, and time to reach customers. The businesses that won were the ones that executed better — better product, better service, better marketing.

AI has removed that barrier. It is not that execution no longer matters. It is that execution is no longer *scarce*. A solo founder with a laptop and a subscription can now produce what used to take a team of five and a six-figure budget. That is a gift — and it is also a trap.

The trap is this: **when execution is cheap and available to everyone, it stops being a differentiator.** If every competitor can build the same website, write the same copy, and run the same ads, then the customer has no reason to choose you over them — except price. And price is a race to the bottom that only the lowest-cost operator wins.

The scarce resource has shifted. It is no longer *what you can do*. It is *why anyone should choose you* — and *why they should keep choosing you as the market moves*.

This is the commoditization problem. This paper is about why it is real, why it is accelerating, and why it is the defining strategic challenge for Singapore small business right now.

---

## 2. The evidence: execution is being commoditized

### 2.1 The cost of starting has collapsed

The most direct evidence is the cost of entry. What used to require capital now requires a subscription.

- A solo professional-services founder can start for roughly **S$2,100 one-time plus S$159–259/month** in recurring costs (Mercury, worked example).
- A two-founder SaaS startup can launch for roughly **S$5,000 one-time plus S$657–1,907/month** (Mercury, worked example).
- Singapore incorporation runs **S$315** (ACRA) with a standard professional-services setup of **S$1,500–3,000 in the first year** (VJM, 2026).

The point is not the exact figures — it is the order of magnitude. **A business that would have required S$50,000–500,000 of capital a generation ago can now be started for the price of a few months of subscriptions.** AI is the reason: it replaces the headcount that used to be the fixed cost.

### 2.2 The supply of new competitors is exploding

When the cost of entry collapses, the number of entrants rises. The Singapore registry data confirms it:

- **77,579 new companies registered in 2025 — up 8.5% year-on-year, or 213 new businesses every day** (ACRA via Emerhub).
- **Half of new businesses do not survive five years** (ACRA: 49.0% five-year survival for the 2020 cohort).
- Low-barrier industries are the most brutal: retail clothing has a **22.6%** five-year survival rate (Emerhub).

Read those together. **213 new competitors every day, and half of them will be gone within five years.** The churn is not a sign of a healthy market — it is a sign of a market where entry is easy and differentiation is rare. Most of those 213 businesses are not failing because they executed badly. They are failing because they had no reason for anyone to choose them over the other 212.

### 2.3 The AI adoption gap is the opportunity — and the threat

Singapore's own data shows the two-speed reality:

- **14.5% of SMEs adopted AI in 2024, up from 4.2% in 2023** — a tripling in one year (IMDA).
- **62.5% of non-SMEs (large businesses) adopted AI** in the same period (IMDA).
- **The gap between SMEs and large businesses grew to 48 percentage points** (IMDA via PwC).
- If Singapore SMEs close that gap, the unlock is estimated at **S$20 billion by 2035** (Public First).

The threat is the gap itself. Large businesses are adopting AI at 4x the rate of SMEs. That means the execution gap between big and small is *widening*, not narrowing — and it is widening precisely because the small businesses that most need the efficiency are the ones least able to capture it.

But there is a second, less obvious reading. **The 14.5% who have adopted AI are the leading edge of a wave that is about to hit everyone.** When the remaining 85% of SMEs adopt AI — and they will, because the grants and the tools are pushing them — the execution floor rises for the whole market. The businesses that differentiated on "we're more efficient" will lose that edge. The ones that differentiated on *position* will keep it.

### 2.4 The demand for differentiation is rising faster than the supply of it

The customer pain is verified, and it is accelerating:

- **50% of Singapore SMEs cite increasing competition as a top challenge — up from 39% in a single year** (QBE Singapore SME Survey 2025, 600 decision-makers). It is the **fastest-rising** challenge on the list.
- **55% cite getting and keeping customers as their top concern** (QBE 2025).
- **Stiff market competition is the #1 concern** for Singapore SMEs (OCBC SME Index, 4Q2025).
- Sentiment is softening into 2026: the **OCBC SME Index registered 51.6 in 1Q2026** (still expansionary, but **22% of respondents expect conditions to deteriorate in the next six months — up 7 percentage points**), and the **SBF Business Sentiment Index fell 2.1 points to 51.3 in Q1 2026**.

The competition number is the one that matters. **In a single year, the share of Singapore SMEs naming competition as a top challenge jumped 11 percentage points — from 39% to 50%.** That is not a blip. That is the commoditization problem arriving, and it is arriving faster than the market can adapt.

### 2.5 The supply of strategy help is not keeping pace

Here is the structural irony. The demand for differentiation is exploding, but the supply of people who can actually deliver it is flat:

- **Management consulting registrations grew only 1.0% in 2025** (ACRA via Emerhub).
- **Business formations grew 8.5% in the same year** (ACRA via Emerhub).

**Demand is outrunning supply by 8x.** The businesses that need to differentiate are multiplying 8x faster than the consultants who could help them do it. That is not a market problem — it is an opportunity. But it is also a warning: the businesses that wait for help will be overtaken by the ones that figure it out themselves.

## 3. Why execution is no longer a differentiator

The previous section showed the *market* evidence. This section explains the *mechanism* — why commoditized execution cannot be a durable advantage.

### 3.1 The three things AI commoditizes

AI has commoditized the three pillars of execution that used to separate winners from also-rans:

| Execution pillar | What it used to require | What it costs now |
|---|---|---|
| **Build** | Developers, designers, months of time | A prompt, a template, hours |
| **Market** | Copywriters, agencies, S$50K+ campaigns | A subscription, a prompt, a fraction of the cost |
| **Serve** | Support teams, salespeople, headcount | A chatbot, an agent, near-zero marginal cost |

Each of these was once a moat. A business that could build faster, market better, or serve more responsively than its competitors had a real advantage. **AI has turned all three into table stakes.** They are now the price of entry, not the reason to be chosen.

### 3.2 The trap: when everyone can do it, price wins

Here is the uncomfortable logic. If every competitor can build, market, and serve at the same near-zero marginal cost, then the customer's decision collapses to one variable: **price.**

And price is the worst possible place to compete, for three reasons:

1. **It is a race to the bottom.** The lowest-cost operator wins, and in a market where AI has collapsed costs, the lowest-cost operator is often a new entrant with no overhead and no standards.
2. **It is copyable in a day.** Your competitor can match your price tomorrow. There is no moat in a number.
3. **It teaches the customer to value you by price.** Once you compete on price, the customer stops seeing your quality, your service, or your position. They see a number — and they will leave for a lower one.

**The businesses that die in the commoditization wave are not the ones that execute badly. They are the ones that compete on execution — because execution is now a commodity, and commodities are won on price.**

### 3.3 The scarce resource has moved

The entire argument reduces to one shift:

> **The scarce resource is no longer *what you can do*. It is *why anyone should choose you* — and *why they should keep choosing you as the market moves*.**

That is the definition of differentiation. And it is the only defensible advantage left, because:

- **It cannot be copied in a day.** A position — the word you own in the customer's mind — takes years to build and cannot be matched by a cheaper competitor.
- **It is not a race to the bottom.** A differentiated business competes on being *chosen*, not on being *cheap*.
- **It compounds.** The longer you own a position, the more the market associates you with it, and the harder it is for anyone else to take it.

## 4. Why the answer goes stale every quarter

This is the part most analyses miss. Differentiation is not a one-time decision. It is a *maintained* position — and the commoditization problem makes it go stale faster than ever.

### 4.1 The mechanism of staleness

A position is "the word you own in the customer's mind." But the customer's mind is not static. Every quarter:

- **New competitors enter** (213/day in Singapore) and claim adjacent words.
- **Existing competitors reposition** — they see your position working and move toward it.
- **The market itself moves** — new customer segments, new needs, new channels.

The position that was right last quarter may be wrong this quarter. Not because you did anything wrong — but because the market moved under you.

### 4.2 The AI acceleration

AI makes this worse in two ways:

1. **It speeds up the competition cycle.** Competitors can now reposition, launch, and market in days instead of months. The window in which a position is "yours" is shorter.
2. **It lowers the cost of copying.** A competitor who sees your position can now replicate the *execution* of it almost instantly. What they cannot replicate is the *ownership* of it in the customer's mind — but they will try, and every attempt erodes your clarity.

### 4.3 The consequence

The consequence is that **differentiation is not a project. It is a subscription to the market.** The businesses that win are not the ones that found a position once. They are the ones that *keep* finding it — that re-run the analysis every quarter, watch the new entrants, and refresh the answer before the market moves past them.

This is the core insight this series will build on: **in a commoditized market, the durable advantage is not a position you found. It is a position you maintain.**

## 5. What this means for a Singapore small business

The evidence in §2 and the mechanism in §3–4 combine into a practical picture. Here is what the commoditization problem means for a real Singapore business owner.

### 5.1 The three failure modes

| Failure mode | What it looks like | Why it happens |
|---|---|---|
| **The price war** | "Competitors are undercutting me. I keep cutting my price to keep customers." | Competing on execution → the customer sees only price → the race to the bottom |
| **The copycat** | "A new player copied my product and is selling it cheaper." | AI lowered the cost of copying; no position to defend against it |
| **The stale position** | "I used to stand out. Now I'm just another option." | The market moved; the position wasn't maintained |

All three are the same disease: **competing on execution instead of position.** The price war is execution without a position. The copycat is a position that was never owned. The stale position is a position that was never maintained.

### 5.2 The one question that matters

Every business owner facing these failure modes is asking the wrong question. They ask:

> *"How do I execute better?"*

The right question is:

> *"Why should anyone choose me — and why should they keep choosing me as the market moves?"*

That is the differentiation question. It is the question this series exists to answer. And it is the question that, answered well and maintained, is the only durable defense against the commoditization problem.

### 5.3 The honest caveat

This paper has argued that differentiation is the only defensible advantage. The honest caveat is that **differentiation is necessary but not sufficient.** A differentiated business still has to execute — it still has to build, market, and serve well. The argument is not "execution doesn't matter." It is "execution is now table stakes, and differentiation is what you win on *on top of* execution."

A business that differentiates but executes badly will fail. A business that executes well but does not differentiate will be commoditized. **The winners do both — and they maintain the differentiation as the market moves.**

## 6. The opportunity

The commoditization problem is not just a threat. It is the biggest opportunity in a generation for Singapore small business — for three reasons.

### 6.1 The gap is the opportunity

Only **14.5% of Singapore SMEs have adopted AI** (IMDA). The other 85% have not. That means the commoditization wave has not fully arrived for most of the market — but it is coming. **The businesses that differentiate *now*, before the wave hits, will own positions that the late adopters cannot take.** First-mover advantage in positioning is real, and it is available right now.

### 6.2 The demand is verified and rising

**50% of Singapore SMEs cite competition as a top challenge, up from 39% in a year** (QBE 2025). The pain is not hypothetical — it is the fastest-rising challenge in the market. The businesses that solve it will be the ones that survive the wave.

### 6.3 The supply is thin

**Management consulting registrations grew 1.0% while business formations grew 8.5%** (ACRA). The demand for differentiation is outrunning the supply of people who can deliver it by 8x. **The businesses that figure out differentiation themselves — or find someone who can do it — will be the exception, not the rule.**

---

## 7. Conclusion

The commoditization problem is real, it is accelerating, and it is the defining strategic challenge for Singapore small business.

- **Execution has been commoditized.** AI has collapsed the cost of building, marketing, and serving — so execution is no longer a differentiator.
- **The scarce resource has moved.** It is no longer *what you can do*. It is *why anyone should choose you*.
- **The answer goes stale.** New competitors enter every day, and the market moves every quarter — so differentiation must be maintained, not just found.
- **The opportunity is now.** The AI adoption gap means the wave has not fully arrived. The businesses that differentiate now will own positions the late adopters cannot take.

**The thesis of this series:** in a commoditized market, the only defensible advantage is a position you own in the customer's mind — and maintain as the market moves. This paper has established *why* that is true. The papers that follow will show *how* to do it.
