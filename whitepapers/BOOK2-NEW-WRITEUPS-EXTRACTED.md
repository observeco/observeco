# Book 2 — New Personal Writeups (rewritten for a layman)

These are the passages I have rewritten for Book 2, in the order they appear in the manuscript. I have written them so that anyone can follow them, even if they have never heard of the businesses or the industries before. I explain every word as I go. My background: I used to work for two companies in Singapore — AlgoMerchant and Two Alpha Pte Ltd. That is where the investing stories below come from. The other stories are about real clients of mine: GreenPackers, Bonefirm, Cai Ca, and a person who wanted to open a salad shop. I have written them as the work they were for me.

### The flank that refused to fight the leader: GreenPackers

Let me start with the simplest words, so we are all on the same page.

**Packaging** is the box, the cup, or the wrapper that food comes in. When you buy a drink, the cup is packaging. When you buy food to take home, the box is packaging.

**Compostable** means the packaging can break down and turn back into dirt, instead of sitting in a rubbish dump forever. **Plant-based** means the packaging is made from plants, not from plastic made from oil. **Home-compostable** means you can break it down in your own garden, not just in a special factory.

**Microplastic** is a tiny piece of plastic, so small you cannot see it. When plastic breaks down, it turns into microplastic, and that gets into the food and the water. **Microplastic-tested** means someone checked that the packaging does not leave tiny plastic pieces behind.

**A distributor** is a company that buys things from a maker and sells them to shops. The maker is called the **manufacturer**. GreenPackers is a distributor. It buys compostable packaging from a manufacturer in Korea, and sells it to shops and restaurants in Singapore.

**Certified** means an outside expert checked the product and said, "Yes, this is true." It is like a report card from a teacher you do not know. GreenPackers has a certificate called **KTR** that says its packaging really is microplastic-tested and home-compostable.

**A rival** is another company that sells the same thing and wants the same customers. **A brand** is the name people know — like Coca-Cola or Nike. **A market** is all the people who might buy something.

**A crusade** is a mission — a cause you fight for with all your heart, the way a knight in old stories fought for a cause. When a business calls itself a crusade, it is saying: "We are not really here to make money. We are here to save the world." That sounds noble, but it can be a dangerous way to talk to customers, as you are about to see.

Now the story.

GreenPackers is a client of mine. It came to me because it was not making money and could not find the right customers. It had a good product — compostable packaging that was tested and certified — but nobody was buying it.

Before I tell you what went wrong, I need to explain one more thing. **A positioning statement** is the one sentence that says what a product is and who it is for. It is the single idea you want customers to remember. **A mind word** is that one idea — the one word you want to plant in customers' heads.

GreenPackers' mind word was a promise it called **"NoP" — No Plastic chemicals, No Paper from Trees.** Every cup and box in its shop carried this. And it split its products into two lines, each with its own slogan: one line was **"It Ain't Plastic"** (cups, straws, bags) and the other was **"Let's Save Trees"** (bamboo cups, paper containers). It also called itself **"Less of Business, More a Crusade,"** and told customers **"we do not strive for profits."** It believed saying all of this would make people trust it more.

That was a bad way to position the business, and it is worth understanding exactly why, because the same mistakes trip up many business owners. There were three problems.

**Problem one — it was two words, not one.** The customer's mind holds one word per brand. GreenPackers was asking it to hold two: "no plastic" *and* "no trees." That is like wearing two name tags that say different things. The customer cannot say what GreenPackers stands for. Is it about plastic? Is it about trees? "No plastic" tells the customer what the product is *not*, but not what it *is* — a negative gives the brain no picture. "No trees" is worse, because trees are good for the earth, so a green company saying "no trees" sounds wrong, as if it were against trees. A confused mind buys nothing.

**Problem two — it was unverifiable.** "No plastic, no trees" was just a slogan. Anyone could copy it. It did not point at GreenPackers in particular, and there was no way to check it was true. A slogan you cannot prove is not a position — it is a wish.

**Problem three — it was a crusade, not a business.** "We do not strive for profits" sounds noble, but to a customer it signals weakness: a company that does not care about money probably will not be around, will not serve you well, and is begging you to buy out of pity. Low-price, please-everyone messaging is the enemy of standing out. And it was a lie anyway — GreenPackers' own prices were higher than the market's, not lower. Its messaging said "cheap crusade," but its prices said "premium, certified." The message and the price were fighting each other.

So GreenPackers came to me with a good product and a broken message. The story of the advice I gave is what matters next.

The market for "compostable packaging in Singapore" is crowded. That means many companies are all trying to sell the same thing. And the people who buy it are careful and doubtful, because many companies lie about being "green" and "eco-friendly." (There is a word for that: **greenwash** — when a company pretends to be good for the earth but is not really. Some sell "biodegradable" plastic that just breaks into tiny toxic pieces.)

The biggest rival was a company called **BioPak**. BioPak is a big, famous brand from another country. It has a website that ranks high on Google, it works with a food-delivery app called **Deliveroo** so thousands of restaurants see its products, and it has hundreds of articles on its website. BioPak is the leader. The leader is the biggest, most famous company in the market — the one everyone knows.

An **SEO agency** is a company that helps other companies show up at the top of Google. An SEO agency would have told GreenPackers: "Fight BioPak. Make your website rank higher than theirs." But that would have been a **frontal assault** — a head-on fight against the biggest, strongest player. That is like a small kid trying to beat the biggest kid in the playground at arm-wrestling. GreenPackers would lose.

So the advice was to do something smarter. Refuse to fight BioPak on BioPak's ground. GreenPackers had a certificate — a proof — that no other company in Singapore had. It is called **microplastic-tested**. Almost every "eco" cup on the market still sheds tiny pieces of plastic into your drink; GreenPackers could prove its cups did not. BioPak is a good company with real certificates, but BioPak does not test for microplastics. That was the one word BioPak could not copy.

So instead of two vague, unprovable slogans, GreenPackers was advised to take **one** word: **microplastic-tested.** GreenPackers could not beat BioPak at "compostable packaging," so the advice was not to try. The advice was to own "microplastic-tested" — a word BioPak could not take, because the proof was not theirs. That is a **flank** — going around the side instead of charging the front. It is not an attack. It is a smarter way to win.

This is the lesson. The leader owns the crowded word — the thing everyone already knows them for. You do not fight for that word. You find the word the leader cannot claim, and you own that one. GreenPackers could not beat BioPak at "compostable packaging," so it was advised not to try. It was advised to own "microplastic-tested" — a word BioPak could not take, because the proof was not theirs.

And here is the hardest, most counter-intuitive part of the whole story, and the reason it belongs in a book about positioning. The advice asked GreenPackers to **give up** "no plastic, no trees." It asked them to let go of a message that sounded true and felt good to say. It asked them to drop the crusade, drop the slogans, and say one thing. That is the thing many business owners struggle with most. They believe that to sell more, they must say more. The truth is the opposite: **less is more**. The word that wins is the one that says one clear thing, not the one that says everything. To own the best word, you must stop caring about the other words — even the ones that feel true and good.

So the irony is this: the path to more — more customers, more money, more of the market — runs through giving up more. GreenPackers was advised to give up "no plastic, no trees." To give up the chance to appeal to everyone. To give up trying to beat BioPak. Even to give up the comforting story that it was a humble crusade. And only by giving all of that up could it have room to own the one word that mattered. That is the counter-intuitive secret: to get more, give up more. The business that refuses to let go of anything ends up holding nothing. Whether GreenPackers took that advice, and what happened if it did, is theirs to tell — my part was the advice.

### The customer who was not being served: Bonefirm

Let me explain the words first.

**A supplement** is a pill or a powder you take to help your body. It is not food, and it is not medicine. It is extra help. **Bone and joint support** means it helps your bones and the places where your bones meet (your joints, like your knees and elbows) stay strong.

**Menopause** is a time in a woman's life, usually around age 45 to 55, when her body changes and stops having monthly periods. It is a normal part of growing older. **Non-hormonal** means the supplement does not use hormones (the chemicals the body makes). **Eggshell membrane** is the thin skin inside an eggshell. It has things in it that can help joints.

**A symptom** is a sign that something is happening in your body. A **hot flash** is a sudden feeling of heat that women can get during menopause. **Brain fog** is when your thinking feels slow and fuzzy.

**A stack** is the group of separate supplements a person takes every day, each for a different thing. **Revenue** is the money a business earns. **Retention** is how many customers keep on buying from you over time, instead of stopping after the first purchase. The word is "retain" — to keep. Good retention means customers keep coming back.

**TCM** stands for Traditional Chinese Medicine — the old, wise way of treating the body with herbs that many Asian families have used for generations. It is very trusted in Singapore.

Now the story.

Bonefirm is a client of mine, founded by a woman named **Shirley Chua**. It makes a supplement for women aged 45 and over, to help their bones and joints stay strong through menopause and beyond. It is made in Singapore, it is non-hormonal, and it is built on eggshell membrane.

**Here is the first thing to understand: Shirley was already good at positioning.** She had got the name and the position right. The name, Bonefirm, says it — firm, strong bones and joints. The position was clear: "firm bones and joints, for women over 45." She was not one of those owners who does not know what their product is for. She knew. That is the part most people get wrong, and she had it right.

So if the position was right, what was the problem? Shirley told me she knew she had to charge a premium price — more than most competitors. A good, made-in-Singapore supplement with a specific purpose costs more than a cheap calcium pill, and she accepted that. But her own numbers were telling her something was wrong. Her **revenue** was not growing the way it should, and her **retention** was weak — customers were buying once and not coming back.

The problem was not the position. The problem was the **reason to purchase** — and it was not sustainable because of price. A woman would look at Bonefirm's price, think, "That is a lot for one supplement," and buy it once out of curiosity — or buy a cheap calcium pill instead. There was no story that made the price feel worth it, again and again. The first purchase was a maybe; the repeat purchase was a no. **That is the gap I was asked to bridge: give the premium a sustainable reason to purchase — an economic reason, not just a nice message.**

Now let me show you what I recommended, and why the numbers change everything.

**The honest problem first.** When you look at Bonefirm against **one** single pill, the price looks crazy high. A cheap calcium pill (like Caltrate) costs about **S$0.40 a day**. Bonefirm costs about **S$8.50 a day**. That is more than 20 times the price of one pill. Any customer who compares Bonefirm to a single supplement will think, "That is far too much to pay for one pill." That was the reason the price felt too high — customers were comparing it to the wrong thing.

**The thing everyone misses is that a menopausal woman does not buy one pill.** Her body changes in many ways at once — her bones, her joints, her skin, her mood. And the market forces her to buy **four or five separate pills**, one for each. That group of pills is the **stack**. And here is the kicker: **she was already paying for the whole stack before Bonefirm existed.**

Let me show you the stack, with real prices I checked at real Singapore shops:


| The concern         | The separate pill she buys      | What it costs per day      |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Bones               | A calcium pill (like Caltrate)  | about S$0.40               |
| Joints              | A joint pill (like glucosamine) | about S$0.50 to S$3.30     |
| Skin and joints     | A collagen drink                | about S$4.50               |
| Mood                | A calming pill                  | about S$0.40 to S$1.50     |
| **The whole stack** | **Four or five separate pills** | **about S$5.80 to S$9.80** |


Add those up and the woman was already spending **about S$5.80 to S$9.80 every day** — buying four or five different supplements from four or five different brands to hit her bones, her joints, her skin, and her mood.

**So the real question is not "is Bonefirm one pill too expensive?" The real question is "Bonefirm is one bottle that replaces four or five — is that fairly priced?"** Bonefirm costs about **S$8.50 a day**. That number sits **inside** the stack's range of S$5.80 to S$9.80. So against the whole stack, Bonefirm is not a mark-up at all. It is **parity** — the same money she was already spending, but now for **one** product that looks after all four things at once. The more pills she was swallowing, the more reasonable Bonefirm's price looks.

This is the **total-cost-of-ownership** idea. When someone says "your price is too high," the question is always: *too high compared to what?* Compared to one cheap pill, S$8.50 looks enormous. Compared to the whole stack she already buys every day, it is exactly what she was paying — for less effort. The comparison to a single pill is the wrong comparison, and it is the comparison that was defeating Bonefirm. That is what turns "too expensive" into "wait, this actually replaces what I already spend."

Then there was a second, cultural anchor. Many Asian women with this problem do not buy Western pills at all. They go to **TCM** — the trusted herbal way — for the same root cause. And I checked what trusted TCM actually costs in Singapore: about **S$7 to S$20 a day**. So Bonefirm's S$8.50 sits in the same band that women already pay, with complete trust, for their bones and joints. The price was not foreign or shocking to the customer — it was *culturally normal*.

This is the fair way to justify a premium. **It is never "cheap."** Saying "Bonefirm is cheap" would destroy it — cheap feels low-quality. The honest words are **"fair"** and **"anchored"**: fair, because it is parity with what she already spends on the stack; anchored, because it sits where she already pays for the trusted traditional solution.

And this is what made the reason to purchase **sustainable**. The first time a woman buys, the message is "one product instead of four." The reason she keeps buying is the same reason she bought her stack every month before — it is not a splurge, it is the same budget, doing more. That is why the retention gap closed: the price stopped being the thing that pushed her away, because it was no longer "expensive." It was "the same money, one bottle."

There was one honest gate we had to test, and I want to be truthful about it. The whole argument rests on one question: **do women actually buy the whole stack — all four or five pills — or only one or two?** If they only buy one cheap pill, then S$8.50 really is four times their cost, and the premium would have to rest on a different reason. So the recommendation came with a test: talk to 20 to 30 real women, ask them what they actually buy and how much it costs, and check the stack is real. If the stack is real, the premium is fair. If it is not, adjust. **The reason to purchase had to be earned by evidence, not asserted by hope.**

The lesson here is different from the others, and that is why I am telling it. **The position can be perfect, and the price can still defeat it.** Shirley got the position right — the name, the customer, the message. What she needed was not a better message. She needed a *reason* the premium was fair — an economic reason that holds up every single month, not a slogan that works once. And the counter-intuitive part is that it came from **spending more, not less**: the more the customer was already paying for the broken-up stack, the more fairly Bonefirm's one-bottle price looked. Sometimes the way to justify a high price is not to make your product cheaper — it is to show how much the customer was already spending on the thing your product replaces.

The reason to purchase was not missing. It was hiding in her customers' own shopping baskets.

### The price that could not buy the word: TradersGPS

Let me explain the words first.

**Investing** is when you put money into something so it can grow. **A stock** is a small piece of a company. When you own a stock, you own a tiny part of that company. **A trading system** is a set of rules that tells you when to buy and when to sell stocks. **A guru** is a person who is famous for being very good at something.

**A brand** is the name people know and trust. **Market share** is the share of customers a company has — how much of the market belongs to it. **A funnel** is the path a customer walks from first seeing a product to buying it. **Online ads** are advertisements you see on the internet.

Now the story.

This is a story from the Singapore investing world. It shows, in the clearest way I know, why price cannot beat a word that is already owned. It is the story of a trading system called **TradersGPS**, told by someone who worked inside the company that priced it — me.

In 2016, a Singapore trading guru named **Collin Seow** and a company called **AlgoMerchant** (where I later worked) built a cloud-based trading system together, called **TradersGPS Everywhere**. AlgoMerchant was a Singapore company that had made its name as a pioneer in computer-driven investing.

The two partners stopped working together in 2018. Then there was a legal fight over who owned the TradersGPS name. It was settled in the Singapore High Court in 2022, with the two sides agreeing to share the name. But the part of the story that matters for this book is not the lawsuit. What matters is what happened in the market while the two sides were fighting. And the lesson is not about the product at all. It is about **who owns the word in the customer's mind.**

Here is the thing that decided everything. In the minds of customers, TradersGPS belonged to **Collin Seow**, not to AlgoMerchant. When people thought of TradersGPS, they thought of the guru — the man with the books, the blog, the reputation, the name they trusted. He owned the word. He owned the market share, and he sold the system for **S$2,888**.

AlgoMerchant had the same system underneath — the same rules, the same product, built together. So the team reasoned: if we sell the same thing for less, we will take his customers. They tried selling at **S$388**. Nothing. They tried selling at **S$38** — a tiny fraction of his price. Nothing. They built a strong funnel, they paid for online ads, they did everything the textbooks say. None of it chipped away at his market share.

Why? Because the customer was not buying a system. The customer was buying the **word** — and the word was owned by Collin Seow, not by AlgoMerchant. It was never about whether TradersGPS was a trading system or a course or a set of signals. It was not about what kind of product it was at all. It was about who the customer believed stood behind it. In the customer's mind, "TradersGPS" meant Collin Seow — his trust, his track record, his name. A cheaper version of the same thing did not change what the customer believed, because the customer was not comparing products. They were choosing the person they trusted. The price did not matter to that choice, because the word was already owned.

This is the lesson the price chapter has been building toward, and it is worth saying plainly. **The battle is never about the product. It is about who owns the word in the customer's mind.** When a competitor owns the word, your lower price does not take their customers. It only tells the market that you are the cheaper version of the thing they already trust — which is not a position, it is a discount on someone else's position. You can undercut a price, but you cannot undercut a word, because the customer is not paying for the thing you are discounting. They are paying for the person, the trust, the name they believe in.

The only way to win is to own a different word, not to sell the same word for less. AlgoMerchant could not become the guru; Collin Seow already was. The price could not buy the word, because the word was not for sale. I saw this failure from inside the company that priced it, and it is the purest example of this chapter's argument that I know. AlgoMerchant owned robo-investing. But TradersGPS — that word belonged to Collin Seow. And no price could change who owned it.

### The research that found the open word: two commissioned studies

Let me explain the words first.

**Bubble tea** is a sweet drink with little chewy balls (called pearls) at the bottom. It is very popular in Singapore. **A chain** is a group of shops with the same name, like McDonald's. **A franchise** is a shop that uses a big brand's name and recipe. **A buyout** is when someone buys a whole business. **A second opinion** is when you ask another expert to check a decision.

**A salad shop** is a shop that sells salads — bowls of fresh vegetables. **A healthy bowl** is a bowl of healthy food, like grains, vegetables, and protein. **The CBD** is the Central Business District — the busy downtown area of a city where offices are. **The heartland** is the residential areas where people live, away from the offices. **White space** is an empty spot in the market — a place where no one is selling, but people want to buy.

Now the story.

It is worth showing what the research I do actually looks like, because the method is the same whether the client is a packaging distributor, a supplement, a bubble tea brand, or a salad shop. Two studies I was asked to do — one for a bubble tea brand and one for the salad market — show the discipline in action.

The first study was for an entrepreneur who was thinking of buying **Cai Ca**, a Singapore bubble tea brand. He had been offered the chance to buy it, and he asked me for a second opinion. Cai Ca had taken over six shops that used to be **Gong Cha** (a big bubble tea chain) after Gong Cha stopped operating in Singapore. Cai Ca was started by a former Gong Cha chief executive.

The question was not whether bubble tea sells — it clearly does. The question was what word Cai Ca could own, and whether that word was open. So I studied the whole category: the trends, the player, the words they each owned.

Here is what the industry looked like. **Premium craft tea** was the category's growth engine — whole-leaf tea and fresh milk, served in beautiful cafes, with a brand telling you it was not really "bubble tea" but a "tea expert." The biggest chain, **CHAGEE**, was scaling fast, calling itself the "tea expert" and aiming to be the "Oriental Starbucks" — tea elevated the way coffee was. Every loud word in the market was already taken. **"The craft and aesthetic"** was taken by CHAGEE and HEYTEA. **"The homegrown local brand"** was taken by LiHO and KOI. **"The indulgent sugar bomb"** was taken by Tiger Sugar and The Alley. **"The cheapest"** was taken by Mixue. **"The healthier coconut drink"** was taken by Mr Coconut. Even **"the old familiar legacy tea"** belonged to Gong Cha, which was about to return in 2026.

The research also found the untaken spaces — the gaps where no one had claimed a word and real demand sat behind it. Those open slots were the reason a buyer would pay for the business at all. But in the interest of my client, I cannot disclose them here.

The second study was for an entrepreneur who is thinking of opening a salad-shop business. He asked whether I could help find the white spaces in the market. So I studied the Singapore salad market from the ground up — the players, the prices, the reviews, the words each one owned.

The market split into two very different territories, and both were crowded in their own way. **The CBD is crowded at every tier** — dozens of salad shops fighting for the same office-worker lunch. And the words were taken. **"Health and wellness"** was taken by SaladStop!, the biggest name. **"Real, seasonal food"** was taken by The Daily Cut. **"Superfood grains with local flavours"** was taken by Grains & Co. **"Best value in the CBD"** was taken by Supergreen. **"Healthy that does not feel like a sacrifice"** was taken by Six Hands. **"The cheap value wrap"** was taken by the heartland chain Stuff'd, and **"the cheapest salad bowl"** was taken by the hawker stalls. Anyone trying to enter "salad" head-on was walking into a wall of already-held words.

The research also found where the market was thin. **Outside the CBD, the premium healthy-bowl tier was nearly empty** — the big CBD names had almost no presence there. The value tier was owned, but the premium bowl that is actually near you, not just near your office, was under-served. And inside the CBD itself there were time-of-day and segment gaps — the dinner crowd, the macro-tracking fitness crowd — that nobody had claimed. Those were the open white spaces. But in the interest of my client, I cannot name them in this book.

The lesson of both studies is the same, and it is the lesson of this chapter. The research did not start with a word the entrepreneur wanted. It started with the market, and it let the market reveal the words that were open. In both markets I could tell you exactly what was taken — the obvious, flattering, crowded words. And the value was precisely what the client needed me not to print — the open word, the empty space with demand behind it, that the incumbents had left open and that my client could actually own. That is what the research is for: to find the word that is empty, that customers want, and that you can actually deliver.
