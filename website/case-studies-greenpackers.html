The engagement

The business Bonefirm — a Singapore-made, non-hormonal bone & joint supplement for women 45+, built on eggshell membrane. Founded by Shirley Chua.
The question The position was already right — "firm bones and joints, for women over 45." But revenue wasn't growing and retention was weak: customers bought once and didn't come back. Why?
The method Positioning Module 1 — competitive set, mind map, pricing landscape, adversarial verification. The premium needed a sustainable reason to purchase.

What the analysis found

1. The position was perfect. The price was defeating it.

Shirley had the name and the position right — "firm bones and joints, for women over 45." The problem was the reason to purchase. Compared to one cheap calcium pill, Bonefirm looked absurdly expensive. A woman would buy it once out of curiosity, or buy a cheap calcium pill instead. The first purchase was a maybe; the repeat purchase was a no.

2. The honest problem: 20× the price of one pill.

A cheap calcium pill (like Caltrate) costs about S$0.40 a day. Bonefirm costs about S$8.50 a day — more than 20 times the price of one pill. Any customer comparing Bonefirm to a single supplement thought, "That is far too much to pay for one pill." They were comparing it to the wrong thing.

3. The thing everyone misses: she doesn't buy one pill.

A menopausal woman's body changes in many ways at once — bones, joints, skin, mood. The market forces her to buy four or five separate pills, one for each. That group of pills is the stack. And here's the kicker: she was already paying for the whole stack before Bonefirm existed.

The whole stack — four or five separate pills, per day

S$5.80–9.80
4. The reframe: parity, not mark-up.

Bonefirm's S$8.50 a day sits inside the stack's range of S$5.80 to S$9.80. Against the whole stack, Bonefirm is not a mark-up at all — it's parity. The same money she was already spending, but now for one product that looks after all four things at once. The more pills she was swallowing, the more reasonable Bonefirm's price looked. That's the total-cost-of-ownership idea: "too high compared to what?"

5. The cultural anchor: TCM.

Many Asian women with this problem don't buy Western pills at all — they go to TCM, the trusted herbal way. We checked what trusted TCM actually costs in Singapore: about S$7 to S$20 a day. Bonefirm's S$8.50 sits in the same band women already pay, with complete trust, for their bones and joints. The price wasn't foreign or shocking — it was culturally normal.

6. Fair and anchored — never cheap.

Saying "Bonefirm is cheap" would destroy it — cheap feels low-quality. The honest words are "fair" and "anchored": fair, because it's parity with what she already spends on the stack; anchored, because it sits where she already pays for the trusted traditional solution. That's what made the reason to purchase sustainable — the same budget, one bottle, doing more.

7. The honest gate we had to test.

The whole argument rests on one question: do women actually buy the whole stack, or only one or two pills? If they only buy one cheap pill, S$8.50 really is four times their cost. So the recommendation came with a test — talk to 20 to 30 real women, ask what they actually buy and what it costs, and check the stack is real. The reason to purchase had to be earned by evidence, not asserted by hope.
